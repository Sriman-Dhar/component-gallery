## Problem

Colour is the cheapest way to add interest, so it gets spent everywhere: a gradient here, a
tinted card there, a coloured icon per feature. Once several saturated colours compete, none
of them mean anything, and the one place colour genuinely needed to work — the primary
action — no longer stands out.

## Mechanism

Build the interface almost entirely from one neutral ramp (a warm or cool grey, chosen and
held). Introduce exactly one saturated accent and treat it as a budget: it marks the primary
action, the current selection, and focus. Everything else — borders, dividers, secondary
buttons, icons, illustration — comes from the neutral ramp. Semantic colours for success,
warning and error are separate from the accent and appear only when reporting state, never
decoratively.

## Why it works

Attention is comparative. A single saturated element in a neutral field is found
pre-attentively, before conscious scanning, so the primary action is located instantly on
any screen. Spending accent on decoration destroys exactly this property. Neutral
discipline also makes the accent swappable, which is what makes the system brandable.

## Visual characteristics

Nine or ten neutral steps doing most of the work. Accent appearing perhaps two or three
times per screen. Borders and dividers as low-contrast neutrals rather than tinted. Hover
states usually shift within the neutral ramp; only the active and focus states reach for the
accent.

## Psychological effect

Reads as confident and expensive. Restraint signals that choices were made — a page using
one colour deliberately looks more designed than a page using five.

## Best used when

Product interiors · anything with a clear primary action per screen · dark interfaces,
where saturated colour is especially loud · brands whose identity should come from type and
structure rather than palette.

## Anti-patterns

Not for products where colour is genuinely categorical — a calendar with per-project
colours, a multi-series chart, a tagging system. There, a full categorical palette is
correct and forcing one accent destroys the information. Also insufficient on its own for
category-heavy consumer products that need warmth and variety.

## Adaptations

The economy principle generalises to any attention-carrying resource: one motion moment per
page, one large type moment per section, one elevated surface per view. Ask what the budget
is, then spend it where the user's decision happens.
